@ 1. Health and Disease - Part 1

According to the WHO definition in the slides, health is:
* A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely the absence of disease or infirmity
- Merely the absence of disease
- Normal vital signs at rest
- The ability to work full-time without sick leave
E: WHO definition quoted in the lecture. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Homeostasis is best defined as:
* The state of balance between body systems necessary for survival and proper function
- A permanent, unchanging internal state
- The process of cell death after injury
- An increase in body temperature during infection
E: The body constantly adjusts to internal and external changes to maintain this balance. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Which organ system is given as the example of water and mineral balance (osmoregulation)?
* The kidneys
- The skin
- The pancreas
- The spleen
E: Slides pair osmoregulation with the kidneys. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

In the slides, glucose regulation is linked mainly to:
* The liver and pancreas (insulin production)
- The kidneys and skin
- The thyroid and thymus
- The spleen and bone marrow
E: Glucose levels regulation: liver and pancreas. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Aetiology means:
* The cause of a disease
- The step-by-step development of a disease
- The structural changes in tissues
- The patient's subjective complaint
E: From the Greek for study of cause; one disease can have more than one aetiology. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Pathogenesis is:
* The series of mechanistic chemical and cellular steps by which a disease develops
- The original cause of a disease
- A laboratory test result
- The final stage of recovery
E: Slides contrast pathogenesis (mechanism/sequence) with aetiology (cause). (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

The latent (incubation) period is:
* The interval between exposure to an injurious agent and the first signs and symptoms
- The period of greatest severity
- The first nonspecific symptoms of onset
- The recovery period after treatment
E: In infectious disease this interval is called the incubation period. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Prodromal symptoms are characteristically:
* Early, often nonspecific, e.g. headache, malaise and fever
- Always organ-specific and severe
- Only visible on imaging
- Absent in acute disease
E: The prodrome marks the first signs indicating onset. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

An acute condition, as classified in the slides, is one that:
* Has relatively severe manifestations but runs a short course (hours, days or a few weeks)
- Lasts for months to years
- Has no signs or symptoms
- Is always congenital
E: Chronic conditions, by contrast, last months to years. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

A sign differs from a symptom because a sign is:
* Objective evidence perceived by the clinician (e.g. a bruise)
- Only felt by the patient (e.g. pain)
- Always measured in a laboratory
- A cause of disease
E: Symptoms are subjective and drive the patient to seek care. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

Pathophysiology includes which four interrelated topics?
* Aetiology, pathogenesis, clinical manifestations and treatment implications
- Anatomy, embryology, histology and biochemistry
- Inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation
- Prevention, screening, treatment and rehabilitation
E: As listed under General Pathophysiological Basis. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

The normal human karyotype described in the slides consists of:
* 46 chromosomes: 22 homologous autosomal pairs plus the sex chromosomes
- 23 chromosomes in total
- 44 chromosomes with no sex chromosomes
- 92 chromosomes in every cell
E: Almost every human cell contains a full diploid genome arranged into 46 chromosomes. (Lecture 1: Health and Disease - Part 1)

In the illness/disease distinction, patients suffer ___ while doctors diagnose and treat ___.
* Illnesses; diseases
- Diseases; illnesses
- Symptoms; signs
- Syndromes; symptoms
E: Illness is the lived experience; disease is the abnormality of structure/function. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Which is listed as an organism response of adaptation?
* Hypertrophy, atrophy and hyperplasia
- Necrosis only
- Fibrosis only
- Metastasis
E: Adaptation is response 1 in the lecture, alongside inflammation, necrosis and regeneration/fibrosis. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

The suffix '-itis' (as in dermatitis) indicates:
* Inflammation
- A tumour
- A bacterial infection only
- Tissue death
E: Slides give dermatitis as the example. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

In acute inflammation, histamine released by mast cells:
* Dilates capillaries and increases leakiness so phagocytes reach the area
- Constricts all vessels permanently
- Stops phagocyte migration
- Lowers body temperature directly
E: Bradykinins alert free nerve endings; phagocytes then consume microbes and debris. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Acute and chronic inflammation are distinguished mainly by:
* Duration and the type of infiltrating inflammatory cells
- The organ affected only
- Whether fever is present
- The patient's age
E: Stated explicitly in the inflammation slides. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Necrosis is defined in the slides as:
* Premature death of cells in living tissue by autolysis (own enzymes, e.g. from lysosomes)
- Programmed, healthy cell renewal
- Growth of new blood vessels
- Reversible cell swelling only
E: Necrosis is response 3 in the lecture. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Primary prevention aims to:
* Prevent the development of disease (e.g. health promotion, education, self-defences)
- Detect disease early by screening
- Restore function after established disease
- Avoid harm from unnecessary medical intervention
E: Secondary = screening/early detection; tertiary = reduce impact of established disease; quaternary = avoid over-medicalisation harm. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Fever (pyrexia) is defined as temperature above the normal range due to:
* An increase in the body's temperature set-point
- Failure of sweating only
- External heating of the skin
- Low hypothalamic activity
E: Internal temperature is sensed/controlled by the preoptic area of the hypothalamus. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Per the temperature classification in the slides, normal temperature is:
* 36.5-37.5 °C
- 35.0-36.0 °C
- 37.5-38.5 °C
- 38.3-40.0 °C
E: Hypothermia is <35.0 °C and hyperpyrexia >40.0/41.0 °C in the same table. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Heat loss by converting water from liquid to gas at skin/respiratory tract is:
* Evaporation
- Radiation
- Conduction
- Convection
E: Radiation transfers to objects, conduction needs contact, convection uses moving fluid. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Nociceptors are:
* Free nerve endings that detect painful (noxious) stimuli and alert the nervous system
- Hormones released in fever
- Platelet precursors
- Thyroid cells producing T3
E: Covered under peripheral aspects of pain / nociceptive system. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

Chronic pain differs from acute pain in the slides principally by:
* Its prolonged duration and persistence beyond the acute episode
- Always having no identifiable stimulus
- Never being assessable by scales
- Being a sign rather than an experience
E: Lecture contrasts acute vs chronic pain and covers subjective assessment scales and pain control. (Lecture 2: Health and Disease - Part 2)

@ 4. Pharmacology

Pharmacology is:
* The science/reasoned study of drugs and substances that modify structures and functions of living organisms
- Only the manufacture of tablets
- Only herbal medicine
- The study of bones and joints
E: Clinical pharmacology examines drug effects in patients; pharmacotherapy is use for a concrete illness. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Every drug's three names are:
* Chemical name, generic name and trade (brand) name
- Latin, Greek and English names
- Oral, topical and injectable names
- Adult, paediatric and geriatric names
E: Example: chemical 2-(4-isobutylphenyl)propanoic acid = generic ibuprofen = trade Motrin. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

The generic (INN) name is:
* The official non-commercial name of a pharmacological substance
- A company marketing name
- The molecular formula
- A patient's nickname for a drug
E: INN = International Nonproprietary Name; paracetamol (BAN) = acetaminophen (USAN). (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Excipients in a pharmaceutical form function to:
* Accompany the active principle and favour its delivery (binders, fillers, disintegrators, lubricants, coaters, flavours etc.)
- Produce the main therapeutic effect
- Replace the active ingredient
- Prevent any absorption in all cases
E: The active principle is responsible for the pharmacological effect. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Bioavailability is:
* The rate and amount of unchanged drug reaching systemic circulation/target from its site of administration
- The colour of a tablet
- The time a drug stays in its box
- Only the IV dose fraction by definition in all routes equally
E: Compared across oral, IV, IM and subcutaneous routes in the slides. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Pharmacokinetics studies:
* What the body does to the drug over time: absorption, distribution, biotransformation/metabolism and excretion
- What the drug does to the body at receptors only
- Only drug naming
- Only adverse reactions
E: Pharmacodynamics is the complementary 'what the drug does to the body'. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Pharmacodynamics includes:
* Physiological/biochemical effects of drugs and their mechanism of action at organ, subcellular and macromolecular levels
- Only excretion by the kidney
- Only tablet disintegration
- Only bioavailability measurement
E: Contrasted with pharmacokinetics in the lecture. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Passive drug transport across membranes includes:
* Simple diffusion and filtration through pores/channels
- Only pinocytosis
- Only carrier proteins against a gradient
- Active transport requiring vesicles only
E: Specialised transport is carrier-mediated or pinocytosis. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Pinocytosis is:
* Transport across the cell in particulate form by formation of vesicles
- Filtration through a pore
- Simple diffusion down a gradient
- Excretion in urine
E: Listed under specialised/carrier transport mechanisms. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Liberation (release) in pharmacokinetics refers to:
* Release of the drug from its pharmaceutical form (tablet, capsule, suppository etc.) before absorption
- Release of hormones from glands
- Patient discharge from hospital
- Removal of a drug from the market
E: It precedes absorption into systemic circulation. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Which is a gaseous pharmaceutical form / route example in the slides?
* Sprays and inhalation
- Suppositories
- Eye drops only
- Syrups
E: Solids include tablets/capsules; liquids include syrups, injectables and eye drops. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

Systemic absorption after topical application depends primarily on:
* Lipid solubility of the drug
- Tablet shape
- The drug's trade name
- Patient height alone
E: Applies to skin, eye, ear, nose, inhalation and mucosal routes. (Lecture 4: Pharmacology)

@ 5. Main Pharmacological Groups

NSAIDs are used to treat:
* Pain, fever and inflammatory processes (antipyretic, anti-inflammatory, analgesic)
- Bacterial infection directly
- Cancer metastasis
- Diabetes only
E: FDA-approved uses as summarised in the lecture. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

The main mechanism of action of NSAIDs is:
* Inhibition of cyclooxygenase (COX), reducing thromboxanes, prostaglandins and prostacyclins from arachidonic acid
- Blocking bacterial cell walls
- Replacing insulin
- Suppressing all lymphocytes permanently
E: Therapeutic effects are attributed to lack of these eicosanoids. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

COX-1, in contrast to COX-2, is:
* Constitutively expressed, maintaining GI mucosa, kidney function and platelet aggregation
- Only induced during inflammation
- Absent from the stomach
- The sole target of celecoxib
E: COX-2 is inducibly expressed during inflammatory response. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Celecoxib is an example of:
* A selective COX-2 inhibitor
- A non-acetylated salicylate
- An antibiotic
- A chemotherapy drug
E: It has a different side-effect profile from nonselective NSAIDs. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Gastric adverse effects of NSAIDs are mainly due to:
* COX-1 inhibition reducing prostaglandins that protect the gastric mucosa
- Increased gastric prostaglandins
- Bacterial overgrowth only
- Low platelet count only
E: Renal, cardiovascular, hepatic and haematologic effects are also listed. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Topical NSAIDs (e.g. diclofenac gel) are noted as useful for:
* Acute tenosynovitis, ankle sprains and soft-tissue injuries
- Systemic hypertension
- Pneumonia
- Leukaemia
E: Relevant to physiotherapy practice. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Bactericidal antibiotics:
* Kill bacteria directly, regardless of the host immune system
- Only inhibit growth so the immune system can eliminate infection
- Only treat viruses
- Are the same as NSAIDs
E: Bacteriostatic agents inhibit growth. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

The first antibiotic, penicillin, was discovered by:
* A. Fleming in 1928
- S. Waksman in 1944
- I. Bacha in 2025
- W. Roentgen in 1895
E: Waksman discovered streptomycin and proposed the name 'antibiotics'. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Antimicrobial agents are:
* Drugs for the treatment and prevention of infectious diseases
- Only painkillers
- Only hormones
- Only imaging contrast agents
E: Antibiotic derives from 'antibiosis' = against life. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

NSAID toxicity can include:
* Anion-gap metabolic acidosis and acute renal failure (often GI symptoms in acute overdose)
- Only hair loss
- Only hyperglycaemia
- No renal effects ever
E: Neurologic toxicity may present with tinnitus. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Which NSAID grouping contains ibuprofen and naproxen?
* Propionic acids
- Acetic acids (diclofenac, indomethacin)
- Enolic acids (meloxicam, piroxicam)
- Anthranilic acids
E: From the chemical-structure/selectivity classification. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

Immunosuppressive drugs, as a group in this lecture, act by:
* Suppressing immune responses (used where dampening immunity is therapeutically required)
- Stimulating antibody production in all cases
- Killing bacteria directly
- Replacing coagulation factors
E: Covered after antibiotics, hormones and chemotherapy drugs. (Lecture 5: Main Pharmacological Groups)

@ 6. Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology

Cancer is defined in the slides as diseases in which there is:
* An uncontrolled process in the division of the cells of the body
- Only benign growth that never spreads
- A bacterial infection of epithelium
- Normal terminal differentiation
E: Malignancy varies with aggressiveness and biological characteristics. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

A benign tumour, compared with a malignant one:
* Does not spread to other tissues and rarely recurs after excision
- Spreads via lymphatics/blood
- Frequently relapses after excision
- Always metastasises
E: Malignant tumours spread and relapse frequently. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Metastasis is spread principally via:
* The lymphatic system or the circulatory system, forming new tumours
- Only direct nerve conduction
- Sweat glands
- The digestive lumen only
E: Also progressive invasion of nearby organs. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Most cancers are thought to arise as monoclonal, meaning:
* A single cell accumulates sufficient mutations in key genes to cause uncontrolled proliferation
- Many cells transform simultaneously with no mutations
- No genetic change is involved
- Only environmental factors matter
E: As cancers progress they may gain mutations and become more heterogeneous. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Angiogenesis in solid tumours is initiated largely by tumour release of:
* VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor)
- Insulin
- Histamine only
- Melatonin
E: VEGF is up-regulated in many tumours; hypoxia increases its secretion. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Tumour vasculature is characteristically:
* Chaotic, irregular, highly permeable, with poor branching/blind ends and lacking normal basement membrane/pericytes
- More regular than normal vessels
- Impermeable to plasma
- Always well innervated
E: This leads to plasma leakage and high interstitial fluid pressure. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

High interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) in tumours:
* Disrupts capillary pressure gradients, reducing blood flow/drug delivery and increasing hypoxia
- Improves chemotherapy delivery
- Lowers hypoxia
- Is caused by strong lymphatics
E: Tumours have leaky vessels and no functional lymphatics. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Chronic tumour hypoxia is mainly due to:
* Limited oxygen diffusion (cells far from vessels become hypoxic)
- Temporary stoppage of flow only
- Anaemia only
- High carbon monoxide only
E: Acute/fluctuating hypoxia is from temporary flow stoppages. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

The oxygen enhancement ratio (OER) concept means hypoxic cells:
* May require up to about 3x more radiation to kill the same number of cells
- Are killed more easily by radiation
- Need no oxygen during irradiation ever
- Are unaffected by oxygen levels
E: Oxygen 'fixes' radiation DNA damage; effect must be present during/right after exposure. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

At least what proportion of cancers is thought preventable, per the slides?
* At least 50% of cases
- Less than 5%
- About 10%
- Essentially 100%
E: Primary prevention focuses on modifiable lifestyle/environmental risk factors. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Cancers arising from epithelium are:
* The most common cancers
- The rarest cancers
- Always benign
- Only found in intestine
E: Rapidly renewing epithelia must escape sloughing/terminal differentiation. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

Physiotherapy in oncology, as introduced in the lecture, must account for:
* Cancer treatments (e.g. chemotherapy/other drug treatments) and immunosuppression in cancer patients
- Only bone density
- Only sports performance
- No treatment side effects
E: Lecture includes cancer & physiotherapy and immunosuppression sections. (Lecture 6: Fundamental of Oncological Pathophysiology)

@ 7. Benign Hematological Pathophysiology

Haematopoiesis is:
* Production of blood cells (erythropoiesis, leukopoiesis, thrombopoiesis) mainly in bone marrow from haematopoietic stem cells
- Destruction of red cells in spleen only
- Clotting of blood after injury only
- Production of hormones by marrow
E: Extramedullary haematopoiesis can occur in liver/spleen. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

The myeloid lineage gives rise to:
* RBCs, granulocytes, monocytes and platelets (innate, first-line defence)
- Only B and T lymphocytes
- Only NK cells
- Only plasma cells
E: Lymphoid lineage gives B, T and NK cells. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Lymphoid cells are key for:
* Adaptive immunity: B-cell antibodies, T-cell cellular immunity, NK killing of abnormal cells, with immune memory
- Only immediate non-specific defence
- Platelet production
- Oxygen transport
E: Myeloid cells drive innate response, inflammation and wound healing. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Anaemia is:
* Lack of enough healthy red blood cells to carry oxygen, causing fatigue, weakness, pallor and breathlessness
- Excess platelets causing clots
- Low white cells only
- Excess red cells thickening blood
E: Erythrocytosis is too many RBCs; leukopenia/thrombocytopenia affect other lines. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

The most common cause of anaemia noted is:
* Iron deficiency
- Vitamin K excess
- Dehydration only
- High altitude only
E: Symptoms may include chest pain, palpitations and frequent infections in the slides. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Thrombocytopenia is:
* Low platelet count, with bleeding risk, from decreased production or increased destruction/use
- High platelet count
- Low haemoglobin only
- Low lymphocytes only
E: Physiotherapy implications/cautions are specifically covered. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Leukopenia is:
* Low white blood cell count, increasing infection risk
- High white blood cell count
- Low red cell count
- A type of lymphoma
E: Contrasted with leukocytosis and discussed vs cancer in the lecture. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Leukaemia develops as:
* A haematological neoplastic disease of white-cell precursors in marrow, affecting blood production and immunity
- A benign iron deficiency
- A platelet-only disorder
- A soft-tissue sarcoma
E: Types covered: ALL, AML, CLL and CML. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Lymphoma is:
* A neoplasm of lymphoid tissue/lymphocytes that can spread through the lymphatic system
- Only anaemia of chronic disease
- A benign platelet disorder
- Inflammation of a single joint
E: Main types, signs/symptoms, diagnosis and treatment are covered. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

In blood cancer, physiotherapy management in the acute phase must consider:
* Physical impairments, fatigue/weakness and treatment phase, adapting exercise and monitoring closely (including palliative care where appropriate)
- Ignoring platelet/WBC counts
- Only maximal strength testing
- No adaptation during chemotherapy
E: Comprehensive and palliative physiotherapy management are explicit sections. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

Neutrophils, as myeloid cells, primarily:
* Attack bacteria and drive inflammation as first-line defence
- Make antibodies
- Produce insulin
- Carry oxygen
E: Macrophages/monocytes engulf pathogens; basophils/eosinophils act in allergy/parasite defence. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

B cells mature/function to:
* Produce antibodies as part of adaptive immunity
- Clot blood
- Transport CO2 only
- Form bone marrow stroma only
E: T cells kill infected cells/regulate responses; NK cells destroy abnormal/virus-infected cells. (Lecture 7: Benign Hematological Pathophysiology)

@ 8. Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems

The musculoskeletal system's bone functions include:
* Support/framework, protection, movement as levers, mineral storage, fat storage, acid-base balance and housing marrow for blood-cell production
- Only protection of the brain
- Only hormone production
- Only digestion
E: Mechanical, synthetic and metabolic categories. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

In adults, red marrow capable of haematopoiesis is restricted mainly to:
* Vertebrae and heads of tibia and femur (with other flat/axial sites), as yellow fatty marrow replaces red marrow with age
- Every long-bone shaft equally
- Only finger bones
- The skull sutures only
E: In infants, marrow of all long bones can synthesise blood cells. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Wolff's Law, as presented, states bone responds to mechanical load by:
* Increasing density/mineralisation with regularly applied load (hypertrophy) and decreasing it when load is reduced (atrophy)
- Never changing after maturity
- Only growing in length
- Losing minerals when exercised
E: Electrical currents: compression negative charge, traction positive charge (piezoelectricity). (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

A fracture is:
* Interruption of bone continuity
- Calcium deficiency without break
- Inflammation of a joint
- Rupture of a ligament only
E: Osteoporosis (calcium deficiency) predisposes to pathological/fatigue fractures. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Absence of a trauma history:
* Does NOT exclude fracture (fatigue/pathological fractures can occur without aggression/trauma history)
- Rules out fracture completely
- Proves osteoporosis
- Means imaging is never needed
E: Clinical signs include deformity, abnormal mobility (not to be provoked) and exposed fracture focus. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Fracture healing management must balance:
* Mechanical aspect (stability) and biological aspect (vascularisation)
- Only immobilisation forever
- Only early maximal loading
- Neither stability nor blood supply
E: Physiotherapy aims include controlling inflammation, minimising adhesions and optimising vascularisation. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Synarthroses are joints that:
* Allow no movement; fibrous joints that absorb loads/shock (e.g. sutures, syndesmoses)
- Are freely mobile synovial joints
- Contain a large joint cavity
- Allow circumduction
E: Amphiarthroses allow slight movement; diarthroses are freely mobile. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

A symphysis is an amphiarthrosis where:
* Thin hyaline cartilage layers separate a fibrocartilage disc from the bones
- Bones are joined only by sutures
- There is a synovial cavity with fluid
- No cartilage is present
E: Synchondrosis joins bones by thick hyaline cartilage. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Diarthroses (synovial joints) are characterised by:
* Free mobility with a synovial joint structure
- No movement
- Fibrous union only
- Direct bone-to-bone fusion
E: Lecture covers joint stability and common joint injuries after classification. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

A sprain is an injury to:
* Ligaments/joint-stabilising structures, graded I-III by severity in the slides
- Only muscle belly
- Only bone cortex
- The skin only
E: Conservative vs surgical treatment is discussed by grade. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Osteoarthrosis, as a common joint pathology in the lecture, involves:
* Degenerative joint change affecting cartilage/joint function and causing pain/stiffness
- Acute bacterial infection only
- Benign bone tumour
- Platelet disorder
E: Listed under common joint injuries and pathologies. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

Physiotherapy after musculoskeletal injury should, per the lecture aims:
* Encourage isometric/isotonic training minimising muscle/joint loss, restore functional ROM and sensorimotor function, and train use of walking aids
- Keep the limb completely inactive indefinitely
- Ignore vascularisation
- Avoid any movement retraining
E: Also control inflammatory signs and minimise adhesions. (Lecture 8: Physiopathology of the Muscle-skeletal Systems)

@ 9. Physiopathology of Digestive System

Ingestion is:
* Entry of food into the alimentary canal through the mouth, where chewing and saliva begin digestion
- Movement of food by peristalsis only
- Absorption in the small intestine
- Elimination of faeces
E: Propulsion/peristalsis then moves food through the tract. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

Peristalsis consists of:
* Sequential alternating waves of contraction and relaxation of alimentary wall smooth muscle propelling food along
- Localised back-and-forth mixing only
- Voluntary tongue movement only
- Gastric churning only
E: Segmentation is the localised mixing contraction. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

Chemical digestion begins in the mouth and involves:
* Digestive secretions breaking complex molecules into building blocks (e.g. proteins into amino acids)
- Only mechanical chewing
- Only water absorption
- Only bile production
E: Mechanical digestion increases surface area/mobility. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

Most enzymatic digestion and almost all absorption occur in:
* The small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum)
- The stomach
- The large intestine
- The oesophagus
E: About 7 m long; bile and pancreatic juice enter the duodenum via the ampulla of Vater. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

In the stomach, the bolus is transformed into:
* Chyme, with gastric emptying regulated by the pyloric sphincter
- Faeces
- Bile
- Amino acids only
E: The stomach completes mechanical digestion and continues chemical digestion. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

The oesophagus moves food mainly by:
* Peristalsis, not gravity, through upper and lower sphincters, crossing the diaphragm at the oesophageal hiatus
- Gravity only
- Ciliary action
- Segmentation only
E: About 30 cm, behind trachea and heart. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

During the pharyngeal phase of swallowing:
* The soft palate closes the nasal cavity and the epiglottis closes the trachea as a reflex peristaltic wave drives the bolus
- The tongue voluntarily pushes food to the stomach
- The pylorus opens
- Bile is released
E: The oral phase is voluntary. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

The liver's digestion-related function highlighted is:
* Producing bile (sent to gallbladder), plus metabolic, toxin/hormone elimination, bilirubin handling, coagulation-factor synthesis and storage roles
- Producing insulin only
- Absorbing most nutrients directly
- Forming faeces
E: Liver receives portal-vein nutrient blood and hepatic-artery oxygenated blood. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

The pancreas exocrine function is to:
* Secrete pancreatic juice into the duodenum via the pancreatic duct, with bicarbonate neutralising gastric acid
- Secrete only insulin into blood
- Store bile
- Absorb fats into lymph only
E: Endocrine pancreas secretes insulin, glucagon, somatostatin and pancreatic polypeptide. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

Intestinal villi and microvilli serve to:
* Provide a huge absorption area (about 300 m² in the slides) in the small intestine
- Produce bile
- Store faeces
- Close the ileocaecal valve only
E: Fats enter lacteal/quiliferous vessels into lymph; other nutrients go via capillaries to the portal vein. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

The large intestine mainly:
* Absorbs water and inorganic ions, forms/eliminates faeces, and hosts bacterial flora synthesising vitamins K and B
- Performs almost all nutrient absorption
- Produces pancreatic juice
- Begins chemical digestion
E: About 1.5 m long, 6.5 cm diameter. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

Faeces are formed from:
* Non-absorbed food remains (e.g. cellulose), intestinal epithelial cells and intestinal bacteria
- Only bile pigments
- Only water and salts
- Undigested bone fragments normally
E: Odour is due to putrid protein fermentation; form depends on colon transit time. (Lecture 9: Physiopathology of Digestive System)

@ 10. Physiopathology of Endocrine System

The endocrine system regulates the body by:
* Glands releasing hormones into the blood to regulate, coordinate and control target organs/cells
- Only nerve impulses
- Only mechanical pressure
- Only lymphatic drainage
E: It is controlled by the nervous system but also has self-regulation. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

The hypothalamus controls the pituitary by:
* Releasing hormones such as GHRH, TRH, CRH and GnRH that start/stop pituitary secretion
- Directly secreting insulin
- Producing melatonin only
- Storing calcium
E: GnRH drives LH/FSH important for puberty. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

The pituitary is called the 'master gland' because:
* It exerts great influence on other organs/glands of the body
- It produces all hormones itself
- It controls digestion directly
- It replaces the hypothalamus
E: Its function is complex and important for general well-being. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

The pineal gland hormone melatonin:
* Helps control circadian/sleep rhythms and can inhibit gonadotropin action
- Raises blood glucose
- Stimulates thyroid T3 release directly
- Increases calcium from bone
E: As summarised in the lecture. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Thyroid hormones T3 (triiodothyronine) and T4 primarily control:
* Metabolism: breaking down/storing food energy and producing waste with energy release
- Only calcium levels
- Only blood clotting
- Only sperm production
E: Thyroid sits in front of the trachea, below the larynx. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) acts to:
* Raise blood calcium (including taking calcium from bone) when calcium is deficient, controlling calcium/phosphorus for bone formation
- Lower blood calcium in all cases
- Secrete insulin
- Produce melatonin
E: Four parathyroids lie behind the thyroid. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

If parathyroids are removed during thyroid surgery, low blood calcium can cause:
* Irregular heartbeat, tingling in hands/feet and possibly shortness of breath
- Only increased muscle mass
- Only high fever
- No symptoms
E: Excess PTH can cause muscle weakness and fatigue. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

The adrenal cortex produces:
* Glucocorticoids (e.g. cortisol) for blood-sugar/stress responses, mineralocorticoids (e.g. aldosterone) for salt/fluid and blood pressure, plus some sex hormones
- Only adrenaline from the medulla
- Only insulin
- Only T3/T4
E: Each adrenal is two organs: outer cortex, inner medulla, above the kidneys. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Aldosterone helps regulate blood pressure by acting on the kidneys to:
* Retain enough salt and fluid, controlling blood volume
- Excrete all sodium
- Lower cortisol
- Produce red blood cells
E: It is a mineralocorticoid. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Negative feedback (negative self-regulation) in the endocrine system means:
* When a hormone level is sufficient, the stimulating gland signal is deactivated/switched off to prevent excess
- Hormones always increase without limit
- The pituitary is destroyed
- Target glands never respond
E: Example given: pituitary-thyroid regulation. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Diabetes mellitus, in the endocrine lecture, is a disorder of:
* Blood-glucose regulation involving insulin (pancreatic endocrine function)
- Only thyroid hormone excess
- Only PTH deficiency
- Only melatonin excess
E: Pancreatic endocrine hormones listed: insulin, glucagon, somatostatin, pancreatic polypeptide. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)

Cushing disease/syndrome and obesity are covered in the lecture as:
* Endocrine pathophysiology topics linked to cortisol/adrenal and metabolic regulation respectively
- Types of anaemia
- Forms of lymphoma
- Grades of sprain
E: Lecture sequence: glands/hormones summary, diabetes, thyroid diseases, Cushing disease, obesity. (Lecture 10: Physiopathology of Endocrine System)
