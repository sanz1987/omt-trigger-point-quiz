# Pathology of Trauma — source: Prof. Renata Kelly da Palma lecture PDFs 1–13 (last year), Basics doc
@ Terminology & imaging
According to the lecture, who published "L'Orthopédie" (1741), the origin of the term orthopaedics?
* Nicolas Andry
- Ambroise Paré
- Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen
- Hugh Owen Thomas
E: Nicolas Andry (1658–1742), professor at the Royal College in Paris, published L'Orthopédie in 1741. He did not practise surgery. (Lecture 1)

The word "orthopaedics" combines the Greek "orthon" with "paidion". What do they mean?
* Straight/correct + children
- Bone + healing
- Joint + movement
- Straight + bone
E: Orthon = straight/correct, paidion = children. (Lecture 1)

Which term means a DEGENERATIVE tendon process?
* Tendinosis
- Tendinitis
- Tenosynovitis
- Enthesitis
E: Tendinitis = inflammatory process in the tendon; tendinosis = degenerative process; tenosynovitis = inflammation of the synovial sheath; enthesitis = inflammation at the insertion point. (Lecture 1)

Inflammation of the synovial membrane that covers a tendon is called:
* Tenosynovitis
- Tendinosis
- Synovitis
- Enthesitis
E: Tenosynovitis = inflammatory process of the synovial membrane covering the tendon. Synovitis refers to the joint synovium. (Lecture 1)

An inflammatory process located at the insertion point of a muscle is called:
* Enthesitis
- Tendinosis
- Bursitis
- Ankylosis
E: Enthesitis ("entesite") = inflammation located at the muscle insertion point. (Lecture 1)

Which term describes an infectious process of bone tissue?
* Osteomyelitis
- Pyoarthritis
- Osteoarthritis
- Synovitis
E: Osteomyelitis = infection of bone; pyoarthritis = joint infection; osteoarthritis = degenerative joint process. (Lecture 1)

Pathological fusion of a joint is called:
* Ankylosis
- Arthrodesis
- Pseudoarthrosis
- Osteosynthesis
E: Ankylosis = pathological fusion. Arthrodesis is the SURGICAL fixation of a joint. (Lecture 1)

How does the lecture define a dislocation?
* Complete and persistent loss of contact between the joint surfaces
- Partial loss of contact with partial congruence
- A ligament injury from excessive stress with momentary subluxation
- Loss of contact plus fracture of the adjacent bones
E: Dislocation = complete, persistent loss of contact between joint surfaces. Partial loss = subluxation; with fractured adjacent bones = fracture-dislocation. (Lecture 1)

"Partial loss of contact of joint surfaces, with partial congruence" defines:
* Subluxation
- Dislocation
- Sprain
- Fracture-dislocation
E: Subluxation = partial loss of contact; capsular and ligament injuries are less severe. (Lecture 1)

Consolidation of a fracture with defective alignment is called:
* Malunion (vicious union)
- Pseudoarthrosis
- Avascular necrosis
- Delayed union
E: Malunion/vicious union = healed in bad alignment. Pseudoarthrosis = non-consolidation with instability at the fracture site. (Lecture 1)

Pseudoarthrosis is best defined as:
* Non-consolidation of a fracture with instability at the fracture site
- Healing of a fracture in poor alignment
- Bone death caused by ischaemia
- Surgical fusion of a joint
E: Pseudoarthrosis = non-consolidation of a fracture characterised by instability of the fracture focus. (Lecture 1)

Death of the cells of bone and bone marrow caused by ischaemia is:
* Avascular necrosis
- Osteomyelitis
- Osteitis
- Osteoporosis
E: Avascular necrosis = death of the cellular component of bone and marrow resulting from ischaemia. (Lecture 1)

Which tendon surgery converts a tendon into a ligament to stabilise an unstable joint?
* Tenodesis
- Tenotomy
- Tenolysis
- Tendon transfer
E: Tenodesis converts a tendon into a ligament (e.g. extensor hallucis tendon fixed to the 1st metatarsal neck). Tenotomy cuts a tendon; tenolysis releases a fibrous tunnel; transfer changes the insertion. (Lecture 1)

Release of a fibrous tunnel after trauma (e.g. trigger finger) is called:
* Tenolysis
- Tenodesis
- Tenotomy
- Z-lengthening
E: Tenolysis = release of a fibrous tunnel, e.g. trigger finger. (Lecture 1)

Moving the insertion of tibialis anterior to compensate for a fibular nerve lesion is an example of:
* Tendon transfer
- Tenodesis
- Tenotomy
- Arthrodesis
E: Tendon transfer = exchange of insertion (e.g. anterior tibial for posterior in a fibular nerve lesion). (Lecture 1)

Union of unstable fractures with metal implants is called:
* Osteosynthesis
- Osteotomy
- Arthroplasty
- Exostectomy
E: Osteosynthesis = union of unstable fractures using metal implants. Osteotomy = removal of a bone portion to correct deviations. (Lecture 1)

A bone graft taken from another person is called:
* Allograft
- Autograft
- Xenograft
- Isograft
E: Autograft = from the same patient; allograft = from another person; xenograft = from another species. (Lecture 1)

On a plain X-ray, which density appears BLACK?
* Gas
- Fat
- Water
- Calcium
E: Gas = black (least absorption, e.g. lungs); fat = dark grey; water = light grey; calcium = white; metal = opaque white. (Lecture 1)

On X-ray, muscles and viscera have which density?
* Water – light grey
- Fat – dark grey
- Calcium – white
- Gas – black
E: Water density (muscles, viscera) appears light grey. (Lecture 1)

In the oblique-view "Scottie dog" sign of the lumbar spine, the pars interarticularis forms the dog's:
* Neck
- Eye
- Nose
- Ear
E: Transverse process = nose, pedicle = eye, inferior facet = front leg, superior facet = ear, pars interarticularis = neck. (Lectures 1 & 8)

In the Scottie dog sign, the pedicle represents the:
* Eye
- Nose
- Ear
- Front leg
E: Pedicle = eye; transverse process = nose. (Lecture 1)

What is the main DISADVANTAGE of a CT scan listed in the lecture?
* High radiation exposure
- Cannot show bone
- Contraindicated with pacemakers
- Very low contrast resolution
E: CT gives high-contrast, cross-sectional and 3D images without superimposition, but its disadvantage is high radiation exposure. (Lecture 1)

Bone scintigraphy shows abnormalities as "hot spots" because:
* The radioactive tracer is taken up by metabolically active bone
- Metal implants reflect the magnetic field
- Sound waves reflect from calcium
- Gas absorbs fewer X-rays
E: A radioactive substance injected IV is taken up by metabolically active bone; increased metabolism appears as hot spots on a gamma camera. (Lecture 1, Seidenberg & Howe 2014)

MRI is contraindicated in patients with:
* Pacemakers, defibrillators or spinal stimulators
- Osteoporosis
- Recent fractures
- Joint effusions
E: Because of the magnetic field MRI is contraindicated with pacemakers, defibrillators and spinal stimulators. (Lecture 1)

Which pair of imaging techniques does NOT use ionising radiation?
* MRI and ultrasound
- CT and ultrasound
- X-ray and MRI
- Bone scintigraphy and MRI
E: X-ray, CT and bone scintigraphy use ionising radiation; MRI and ultrasound do not. (Lecture 1 summary)

Which imaging method can evaluate structures DYNAMICALLY at relatively low cost and without radiation?
* Ultrasound
- MRI
- CT scan
- Bone scintigraphy
E: Musculoskeletal ultrasound uses sound waves, has no radiation risk, is low cost and can evaluate structures dynamically. (Lecture 1)

@ Soft tissue & fractures
A muscle strain is defined in the lecture as:
* An acute or chronic injury of contractile components (muscles and tendons)
- Damage to one or more ligaments of a joint
- A haematoma caused by damaged capillaries
- A fracture of a muscle insertion
E: Strain (muscle tear/pulled muscle) = injury of contractile components; sprain = ligament damage; contusion = bruise. (Lecture 2)

A sprain is damage to:
* One or more ligaments of a joint
- Muscle fibres
- Tendons only
- Capillaries under the skin
E: Sprain = ligament damage when the joint is taken beyond its functional range. (Lecture 2)

Which strain grade presents with NO movement and severe pain?
* Third degree
- First degree
- Second degree
- Grade 0
E: First degree = little tearing, pain with full ROM; second = torn tissue, painful limited motion; third = most severe, no movement. (Lecture 2)

Which list contains skeletal functions given in the lecture?
* Support, movement, protection, mineral reserve, haemopoiesis
- Support, digestion, protection, thermoregulation
- Movement, hormone storage, filtration, haemopoiesis
- Protection, insulation, excretion, support
E: Support, movement (through joints), protection, mineral reserve and haemopoiesis. (Lecture 2)

Which fracture sign "should NOT be researched" (tested) according to the lecture?
* Abnormal mobility
- Pain
- Bruising
- Deformity
E: Abnormal mobility is a fracture sign that should not be provoked during examination. (Lecture 2)

The absence of a history of trauma:
* Does NOT exclude a fracture
- Excludes a fracture
- Means the fracture is open
- Means the fracture is stable
E: Fatigue or pathological (e.g. osteoporotic) fractures occur without aggression – absence of trauma does NOT exclude fracture. (Lecture 2)

A closed fracture that is complicated by significant soft-tissue injury is correctly termed:
* Complex fracture
- Compound fracture
- Simple fracture
- Greenstick fracture
E: Open = compound (skin breached). A closed fracture complicated by soft-tissue injury = complex fracture. (Lecture 2)

Which fracture patterns does the lecture list as UNSTABLE?
* Oblique, spiral, comminuted or double fractures
- Incomplete and transverse fractures
- Greenstick and torus fractures
- Avulsion and impacted fractures only
E: Stable: incomplete and transverse (held by muscle elastic contraction after reduction). Unstable: oblique, spiral, comminuted or double. (Lecture 2)

Displacement of a fracture is described by the position of:
* The distal fragment relative to the proximal bone
- The proximal fragment relative to the distal bone
- The fracture line relative to the joint
- The bone relative to the skin
E: Displacement is defined by the abnormal position of the distal fragment in relation to the proximal bone. (Lecture 2)

A fracture in which one fragment is driven into another (e.g. distal radius) is:
* Impacted
- Avulsion
- Spiral
- Greenstick
E: Impacted fracture = a bone fragment driven into another fragment. (Lecture 2)

Why do intra-articular fractures matter?
* Risk of post-traumatic osteoarthritis
- They never need fixation
- They heal faster than diaphyseal fractures
- They are always open
E: The lecture notes intra-articular fractures carry a risk of post-traumatic OA. (Lecture 2)

Which two factors influence the time of fracture consolidation?
* Mechanical (stability) and biological (vascularisation)
- Patient height and limb length
- Skin colour and temperature
- Type of X-ray and projection
E: There are no strict rules; the mechanical aspect (stability) and biological aspect (vascularisation) influence consolidation. (Lecture 2)

The main difference between osteitis and osteomyelitis is that osteomyelitis spreads:
* Centrifugally, starting as an infection of the bone marrow
- Centripetally, from the soft tissue inward
- Only through the joint
- Only after surgery
E: Osteitis = centripetal; osteomyelitis = centrifugal (primary infection of the bone marrow, then cortex and periosteum). (Lecture 2)

In compartment syndrome, the FIRST pathogenic event is:
* Tissue pressure exceeds venous pressure, impairing outflow
- Arterial occlusion
- Ischaemic necrosis
- Renal failure
E: Sequence: tissue pressure > venous pressure → arterial compromise → neural/muscle dysfunction → ischaemic necrosis/contracture → (severe) renal failure & death. (Lecture 2)

After roughly how long without blood supply does progressive muscle necrosis begin in compartment syndrome?
* 6–8 hours
- 30 minutes
- 2 days
- 1 week
E: Muscle tolerates short periods of hypoxia but after a few hours (6–8 h) progressive necrosis begins. (Lecture 2)

What is the treatment of compartment syndrome?
* Surgical dermatofasciotomy (release of the compartment)
- Tight compression bandage
- Elevation and ice only
- Cast immobilisation
E: Treatment = release of the closed osteofascial compartment by surgical dermatofasciotomy. (Lectures 2 & 11)

Torus (buckle) fractures typically involve:
* The distal radial metaphysis in children after a fall on an outstretched arm
- The femoral neck in the elderly
- The fifth metatarsal neck after punching
- The vertebral body in osteoporosis
E: Torus/buckle fractures = incomplete fractures with cortical bulging from axial loading, frequently the distal radial metaphysis in children. (Lecture 2)

Physeal (growth plate) fractures make up what proportion of children's fractures?
* 15–30%
- 1–2%
- 50–60%
- 80–90%
E: Physeal fractures are common, 15–30% of all fractures in children. (Lecture 2)

An insufficiency fracture occurs when:
* Osteoporotic bone is subjected to normal stress
- Normal bone is subjected to abnormal activity
- A tumour destroys the cortex
- A muscle avulses a bony fragment
E: Insufficiency fracture = abnormal (osteoporotic) bone + normal stress; stress/fatigue fracture = normal bone + abnormal activity (overuse). (Lecture 2)

The most common sites of stress fractures in the foot are the:
* 2nd and 3rd metatarsals
- 1st and 5th metatarsals
- Talus and calcaneus
- Phalanges
E: The 2nd and 3rd metatarsals (greatest impact during push-off). (Lecture 2)

Tibial stress fractures in runners account for up to:
* Half of all stress fractures
- 5% of stress fractures
- 90% of stress fractures
- None – they are rare
E: Tibial stress fractures are a common overuse injury in runners, accounting for up to half of all stress fractures. (Lecture 2)

The "female athlete triad" associated with stress fractures consists of:
* Amenorrhoea, disordered eating and osteoporosis
- Obesity, diabetes and hypertension
- Anaemia, fatigue and tendinopathy
- Hypermobility, scoliosis and flat feet
E: A stress fracture may herald underlying amenorrhoea, disordered eating and osteoporosis. (Lecture 2)

The main management of most stress fractures is:
* Rest from high-impact activity (conservative)
- Immediate ORIF
- Amputation
- Continued training through pain
E: Most stress fractures are managed conservatively with rest; high-risk displaced ones need surgery. (Lecture 2)

@ Shoulder
Which rotator cuff muscle is the most commonly ruptured?
* Supraspinatus
- Infraspinatus
- Teres minor
- Subscapularis
E: SITS muscles; supraspinatus is the most commonly ruptured. (Lecture 3)

Most clavicle fractures (94%) result from:
* A direct blow on the shoulder, generally a fall on the side
- A fall on an outstretched hand
- Repetitive overhead throwing
- A twisting injury of the arm
E: 94% result from a direct blow on the shoulder, generally falling on the side. (Lecture 3, McRae)

Why are fractures of the middle third of the clavicle most common?
* It is the thinnest part and not reinforced by muscles or ligaments
- It is the thickest part of the bone
- The sternocleidomastoid inserts there
- It is where the growth plate is located
E: The middle is the thinnest part and the only part not reinforced by muscles and ligaments. (Lecture 3)

In adults, the medial fragment of a displaced clavicle fracture is pulled upward by:
* Sternocleidomastoid
- Pectoralis major
- Trapezius
- Deltoid
E: Weight of the limb pulls the distal fragment down; SCM pulls the medial fragment up. (Lecture 3)

According to the AAOS guidance in the lecture, displaced midshaft clavicle fractures in adults:
* Are recommended for surgical fixation
- Should always be treated with a figure-of-eight brace
- Should be treated with LIPUS
- Need no immobilisation
E: Displaced midshaft (adults): surgical fixation recommended (higher union, better early outcomes). Simple sling preferred over figure-of-eight; LIPUS not recommended. (Lecture 3)

In the Neer classification, a "part" is considered displaced if there is:
* >1 cm separation or >45° angulation
- >2 mm separation
- >5 cm separation or >10° angulation
- Any visible fracture line
E: Neer: displaced part = >1 cm separation or >45° angulation. (Lecture 3)

Which Neer category accounts for about 75% of proximal humerus fractures?
* One-part
- Two-part
- Three-part
- Four-part
E: One-part ~75%, two-part ~20%, three-part ~5%, four-part <1%. (Lecture 3)

Which proximal humerus fracture type carries a high risk of avascular necrosis?
* Four-part
- One-part
- Two-part surgical neck
- Greater tuberosity avulsion
E: Four-part: all parts displaced, articular surface lateral and out of contact with glenoid – high AVN risk. (Lecture 3)

What are Codman's four "parts" of the proximal humerus?
* Articular surface, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, humeral shaft
- Head, neck, shaft, condyles
- Greater tuberosity, glenoid, acromion, coracoid
- Anatomical neck, surgical neck, shaft, epicondyle
E: Articular surface, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity and shaft. (Lecture 3)

For an elderly, active patient with a displaced 3- or 4-part proximal humerus fracture, the lecture suggests:
* Reverse shoulder arthroplasty
- ORIF
- No treatment
- Figure-of-eight brace
E: Decision summary: young/fit → ORIF; elderly active → RSA; frail/low demand → conservative. (Lecture 3)

Which nerve is most commonly injured in humeral shaft fractures?
* Radial nerve
- Axillary nerve
- Ulnar nerve
- Musculocutaneous nerve
E: The radial nerve runs in the spiral groove – the most common associated injury. Most recover in 3–6 months. (Lecture 3)

Anterior proximal humerus fracture-dislocations may injure the:
* Axillary nerve
- Radial nerve
- Median nerve
- Long thoracic nerve
E: Anterior fracture-dislocations may injure the axillary nerve. (Lecture 3)

Which joint suffers the most dislocations in the body?
* Shoulder (about 45%)
- Knee
- Hip
- Elbow
E: Shoulder = 45% of all dislocations; 84% anterior glenohumeral. (Lecture 3)

In type III acromioclavicular dislocation:
* The coracoclavicular (trapezoid and conoid) ligaments are ruptured
- Only the AC ligament is sprained
- There is no ligament damage
- The sternoclavicular joint is dislocated
E: Type I = sprain; Type II = AC ligament torn + CC sprain; Type III = CC ligaments ruptured. (Lecture 3)

Which is a DYNAMIC shoulder stabiliser?
* Rotator cuff muscles
- Glenoid labrum
- Inferior glenohumeral ligament
- Negative intra-articular pressure
E: Static: capsular ligaments, labrum, negative pressure. Dynamic: rotator cuff, long head of biceps, deltoid, pectoralis major. (Lecture 3)

The acronym TUBS stands for:
* Traumatic, Unidirectional, Bankart, Surgery
- Traumatic, Universal, Bilateral, Splint
- Tendon, Unstable, Bursitis, Sling
- Torn, Unilateral, Biceps, Strengthening
E: TUBS = Traumatic Unidirectional Bankart Surgery; first episode usually anterior. (Lecture 3)

The acronym AMBRI describes instability that is:
* Atraumatic, Multidirectional, Bilateral, treated with Rehabilitation, Inferior capsular shift
- Acute, Medial, Bony, Reduced, Immobilised
- Anterior, Mechanical, Bankart, Repair, Imaging
- Acquired, Microtrauma, Biceps, Rest, Injection
E: AMBRI – mostly due to laxity; good prognosis with conservative rehabilitation. (Lecture 3)

A Hill-Sachs lesion is:
* A posterolateral humeral head compression fracture
- Detachment of the anteroinferior labrum
- A superior labral tear from anterior to posterior
- A fracture of the coracoid
E: Hill-Sachs = posterolateral humeral head compression fracture; Bankart = anteroinferior labral detachment; SLAP = superior labrum. (Lecture 3)

AIOS instability results from:
* Repetitive microtrauma (acquired instability from overstress)
- A single high-energy trauma
- Congenital hypermobility only
- Fracture of the glenoid
E: AIOS = Acquired Instability Overstress (Surgery may be needed) – repetitive microtrauma in occupational/sports activities. (Lecture 3)

@ Elbow
Which muscle is MOST predominantly involved in tennis elbow?
* Extensor carpi radialis brevis
- Pronator teres
- Brachioradialis
- Flexor carpi ulnaris
E: Lateral epicondylitis: ECRB most predominantly, also ECRL, extensor digitorum communis, ECU. (Lecture 4)

What proportion of tennis elbow patients do NOT play sports?
* 95%
- 10%
- 50%
- 5%
E: High incidence (35–55 y); 95% of patients do not practise sports. (Lecture 4)

Golfer's elbow pain is aggravated by:
* Resisted wrist flexion and pronation
- Resisted wrist extension and supination
- Passive elbow flexion
- Shoulder abduction
E: Medial epicondylitis is aggravated by resisted wrist flexion and pronation. (Lecture 4)

Pathophysiologically, epicondylitis is described as:
* A chronic tendinosis with disorganised tissue and neovessels
- An acute bacterial infection
- A bone fracture
- A nerve entrapment
E: Epicondylitis represents a chronic tendinosis rather than an acute inflammatory process. (Lecture 4)

What percentage of epicondylitis cases can be managed non-operatively?
* Over 90%
- About 25%
- About 50%
- Under 10%
E: Over 90%; surgery only after ≥6 months of failed non-operative care. Eccentric strengthening is effective. (Lecture 4)

Counterforce braces for epicondylitis are placed approximately:
* 6–10 cm distal to the elbow joint
- On the wrist
- Above the elbow
- 20 cm distal to the elbow
E: Placed on the forearm about 6–10 cm distal to the elbow joint. (Lecture 4)

Cubital tunnel syndrome is entrapment of which nerve?
* Ulnar nerve
- Median nerve
- Radial nerve
- Musculocutaneous nerve
E: The cubital tunnel lets the ulnar nerve pass from arm to forearm; paraesthesia in digits 4 and 5. (Lecture 4)

The most common elbow fracture in children (peak 5–7 years) is:
* Supracondylar humerus fracture
- Radial head fracture
- Olecranon fracture
- Coronoid fracture
E: Supracondylar fracture – mechanism: fall on a hyperextended elbow. (Lecture 4)

Volkmann's contracture after a supracondylar fracture is caused by:
* Ischaemic injury to the brachial artery
- Radial nerve palsy
- Malunion in varus
- Infection
E: Volkmann's contracture = ischaemic injury of the brachial artery causing permanent flexion of hand and wrist. Cubitus varus (gunstock) is the malunion. (Lecture 4)

The "gunstock deformity" after a supracondylar fracture is:
* Cubitus varus
- Cubitus valgus
- Volkmann's contracture
- Dinner fork deformity
E: Cubitus varus malunion = gunstock deformity. (Lecture 4)

Radial head fractures constitute about what proportion of elbow fractures?
* 33%
- 5%
- 75%
- 90%
E: 33%; mechanism: FOOSH with elbow extended and forearm pronated; often subtle on X-ray. (Lecture 4)

A Mason type IV radial head fracture is:
* A radial head fracture with associated elbow dislocation
- Non-displaced <2 mm
- Partial articular displacement >2 mm
- Comminuted without dislocation
E: Mason I non-displaced; II partial >2 mm; III comminuted; IV with elbow dislocation. (Lecture 4)

A Monteggia fracture is:
* Fracture of the proximal/middle ulna with dislocation of the radial head
- Fracture of the distal radius with DRUJ dislocation
- Fracture of the radial head and coronoid with elbow dislocation
- Transverse fracture of the 5th metacarpal neck
E: Monteggia = ulna fracture + radial head dislocation (peak 4–10 y). Galeazzi = distal radius + DRUJ. (Lecture 4)

The terrible triad of the elbow consists of:
* Elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture
- ACL + MCL + medial meniscus injury
- Olecranon + radial head + ulnar nerve injury
- Supracondylar fracture + brachial artery + median nerve injury
E: Terrible triad = elbow dislocation, radial head fracture and coronoid fracture (often collateral ligament injury). (Lecture 4)

The elbow is the most commonly dislocated joint in:
* Children
- Elderly women
- Adult athletes
- Newborns
E: Elbow dislocation = most common in children, second most common in adults; mostly posterolateral after FOOSH. (Lecture 4)

Which fracture is the 3rd most common in children and associated with elbow dislocation in 50% of cases?
* Medial epicondyle fracture
- Supracondylar fracture
- Radial neck fracture
- Olecranon fracture
E: Medial epicondyle fracture – 50% associated with elbow dislocation; caused by valgus avulsion. (Lecture 4)

@ Wrist & hand
Carpal tunnel syndrome is entrapment of the:
* Median nerve
- Ulnar nerve
- Radial nerve
- Posterior interosseous nerve
E: CTS = median nerve entrapment; tests: Phalen, reverse Phalen, Tinel, EMG/nerve conduction. (Lecture 5)

De Quervain's tenosynovitis affects the tendons of:
* Extensor pollicis brevis and abductor pollicis longus
- Flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus
- Extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis
- Extensor indicis and extensor digitorum

Dupuytren's contracture is:

- Avascular necrosis of the lunate
- Tenosynovitis of the thumb

A Colles' fracture is:

- Distal radius fracture with ANTERIOR displacement after a fall on a flexed wrist
- Distal radius fracture with DRUJ dislocation
- Fracture at the base of the 1st metacarpal
E: Colles' = dorsal deviation, "dinner fork" deformity, ulnar styloid fracture in >60%. Smith's = reverse (anterior). (Lecture 5)

Smith's fracture results from:
* A fall onto a flexed wrist, with anterior displacement
- A fall onto an outstretched hand, with dorsal displacement
- Punching a wall
- Forced thumb abduction
E: Smith's (reverse Colles') = fall onto flexed wrist, anterior deviation; less common than Colles'. (Lecture 5)

The most common carpal fracture is the:
* Scaphoid
- Triquetrum
- Lunate
- Hamate
E: Scaphoid = most common (anatomical snuffbox tenderness); triquetrum = 2nd (15%). (Lecture 5)

Which part of the scaphoid has the highest risk of non-union/AVN?
* Proximal pole (~30–40% non-union)
- Distal pole
- Waist
- Tubercle
E: Distal pole ~100% union; waist 10–20% non-union; proximal pole 30–40%. (Lecture 5)

Kienböck's disease is avascular necrosis of the:
* Lunate
- Scaphoid
- Femoral head
- Capitate
E: Kienböck's = AVN of the lunate; negative ulnar variance may contribute. (Lecture 5)

A Bennett fracture is:
* An intra-articular two-part fracture-dislocation at the base of the 1st metacarpal
- A comminuted (≥3 parts) fracture at the base of the 1st metacarpal
- A transverse fracture of the 5th metacarpal neck
- A distal radius fracture
E: Bennett = two-part; Rolando = comminuted (≥3 parts); both from forced abduction. (Lecture 5)

A boxer's fracture is a fracture of the:
* Neck of the 5th metacarpal
- Base of the 1st metacarpal
- Scaphoid waist
- Distal phalanx
E: Boxer's fracture = transverse fracture of the 5th metacarpal neck; most common metacarpal fracture. (Lecture 5)

A Galeazzi fracture is:
* Distal radius fracture with dislocation of the distal radioulnar joint
- Proximal ulna fracture with radial head dislocation
- Scaphoid fracture with lunate dislocation
- Distal radius fracture with dorsal angulation only
E: Galeazzi = distal radius + DRUJ dislocation (fall on outstretched hand with flexed elbow). (Lecture 5)
