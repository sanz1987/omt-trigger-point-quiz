Travell's definition describes a trigger point as a hyper-irritable spot in skeletal muscle associated with:
* A hypersensitive palpable nodule in a taut band
- An inflamed bursa
- A torn tendon
- A calcified ligament
E: "A hyper-irritable spot in skeletal muscle associated with a hypersensitive palpable nodule in a taut band…" with referred pain, motor dysfunction and autonomic phenomena. (TrP Part 1)

The lecture compares trigger-point referral to a leaky water pistol because:
* Referral can include the area between the trigger point and the distant painful point
- The pain is always wet and burning
- Trigger points leak fluid
- Pain only appears exactly at the trigger point
E: The pistol wets a distant point and often everything in between. (TrP Part 1 / study pack)

Who coined the term "myofascial trigger point" in the 1950s?
* Travell and Rinzler
- Balfour
- Froriep
- Kellgren
E: Balfour 1816 "nodular tumours", Froriep 1843 "muskelschwiele", Schade 1919 "myogeloses", Kellgren saline maps, Travell & Rinzler 1950s "myofascial trigger point". (TrP Part 1)

Which type of trigger point is the most painful and usually located at the neuromuscular (innervation) point?
* Central/primary
- Satellite/secondary
- Latent
- Attachment
E: Central/primary = most painful, at the innervation point ("knot"). Satellites form in the referral zone of a central TrP. (TrP Part 1)

Satellite trigger points usually:
* Do not respond until the primary trigger point is treated
- Are treated before the primary
- Never become active
- Are found only in tendons
E: Satellites form in response to a central TrP within its referral zone; treat the primary first. (TrP Part 1)

A latent trigger point:
* Feels like a TrP but is not painful and does not refer on palpation
- Refers pain spontaneously
- Is always the most painful point
- Cannot become active
E: Latent TrPs are not painful/referring but can become active after injury or if their primary activates. (TrP Part 1)

Which classic theory explains trigger points through muscle spindles?
* A sensitised spindle fires continuously and does not let the fibre lengthen, forming a spasm
- Spindles calcify inside tendons
- Spindles release lymph into the muscle
- Golgi tendon organs tear
E: Muscle-spindle theory: sensitised spindle (injury, overload, sustained contraction) → continuous firing → spasm/TrP. (TrP Part 2)

Ziembicki (2023) proposed that trigger points are located at:
* Nerve Entry Points along the course of a nerve
- Musculotendinous junctions only
- Lymph nodes
- Joint capsules
E: Myofascial TrPs connect to distinct points along a nerve (NEPs) – anatomical basis. (TrP Part 2)

Quintner and Cohen's critique (1994) suggested referred pain in "trigger points" may come from:
* Peripheral nerve irritation rather than an injury in muscle tissue
- Bone marrow inflammation
- Joint capsule tears
- Fascial calcification
E: They proposed peripheral neural irritation/inflammation or ischaemia; modern reviews use a multifactorial model. (TrP Part 1–2)

Wolfe et al. (1992) found that:
* Even leading experts cannot reliably locate trigger points
- Trigger points are always harder on algometry
- Dry needling is always superior
- Trigger points never refer pain
E: Experts could not accurately and reliably locate TrPs. Andersen 2010 found the "hard" spots were softer on algometry. (TrP Part 2)

Sprenger et al. (2011) attribute pain reduction after painful treatments such as dry needling to:
* Diffuse noxious inhibitory control plus expectation and placebo
- Mechanical release of contracture only
- Calcium removal from the muscle
- Lymphatic drainage
E: DNIC and non-specific psychological effects (expectation, placebo). (TrP Part 2)

Which autonomic change is "pilomotor"?
* Goosebumps or hair loss/gain
- Sweaty, shiny skin
- Circulatory changes
- Pupil dilation
E: Sudomotor = sweaty/shiny skin; vasomotor = circulation; pilomotor = hair loss/gain, goosebumps. (TrP Part 1)

During manual pressure release, the patient's pain on the NRS should be no more than:
* 5–6/10
- 1–2/10
- 8–9/10
- 10/10
E: Moderate pressure; pain no more than 5–6/10. (TrP Part 1)

What is the maximum hold time for trigger-point pressure in the course method?
* 10 seconds or until the pain eases
- 2 minutes
- 30 seconds minimum
- 5 minutes
E: Maintain pressure until pain eases, maximum 10 s, then rest and repeat about 3 times (2–4). (TrP Part 1)

When should you stop trigger-point pressure?
* If the pain increases
- After exactly 4 repetitions regardless
- When the patient breathes out
- Never – continue until numbness
E: "Stop if the pain increases!!!" (TrP short version)

Which is listed as a PRECAUTION (not a contraindication) for trigger-point therapy?
* Pregnancy
- Malignancy
- Open wound
- Recent burn to the area
E: Precautions: high pain, hypersensitivity, anxiety, acute healing, pregnancy, epilepsy, anticoagulants. Malignancy, open wounds and recent burns are contraindications. (TrP Part 1)

Lew et al. (2021) meta-analysis compared dry needling with trigger-point manual therapy for neck/upper-back pain and found:
* They were equal for VAS, PPT and NDI in the short to medium term
- Dry needling was far superior
- Manual therapy was harmful
- Neither had any effect
E: DN and TrP manual therapy were equal. (TrP Part 1 / study pack)

Zhou et al. (2023) found that adding which component to clinical TrP treatment gave better pain, ROM and function?
* Exercise
- Ice
- Rest
- Injection
E: Adding exercise improves outcomes more than a single treatment. (TrP Part 1)

The most common sites for trigger-point injections listed in the slides include:
* Masseter, levator scapulae, gluteus medius, QL, trapezius, SCM, temporalis
- Biceps, triceps, deltoid
- Hamstrings, gastrocnemius, soleus
- Rectus abdominis, obliques, diaphragm
E: Hammi et al. 2023 list. (TrP Part 2)

The piriformis is called a "double devil" (Travell & Simons) because it:
* Causes distress both by nerve entrapment and by projecting pain from trigger points
- Has two heads
- Refers pain to both arms
- Is innervated by two nerves
E: Quote in TrP Part 2; Lewis 2016 found the sciatic nerve passed beneath piriformis in 89% of limbs. (TrP Part 2)

@ Mulligan concept
The Mulligan concept was developed by:
* Brian Mulligan, a New Zealand physiotherapist
- Geoffrey Maitland, an Australian physiotherapist
- Fred Mitchell Sr, an osteopath
- Emil Vodder, a Danish therapist
E: Brian Mulligan (NZ), influenced mainly by Kaltenborn; taught internationally from 1972. (Mulligan lecture)

Mulligan's core idea is that pain is related to:
* Positional faults of joints
- Lymphatic congestion
- Trigger points
- Fascial adhesions
E: Key idea: "positional faults". (Mulligan lecture)

A Mobilisation With Movement combines:
* A therapist-applied accessory glide with a patient-generated active movement
- A passive physiological movement at end range
- High-velocity thrust manipulation
- Isometric contraction followed by stretch
E: MWM = therapist joint mobilisation + patient active movement to achieve pain-free function. (Mulligan lecture)

What does the PILL response stand for?
* Pain-free, Instant, Long Lasting
- Pressure, Isometric, Lengthen, Load
- Passive, Intermittent, Light, Localised
- Pain, Inflammation, Laxity, Limitation
E: If there is no PILL response you do not use the technique. (Mulligan lecture)

In the CROCKS rules, how many repetitions are suggested?
* 3–10, depending on irritability and response
- 1 only
- 20–30
- 50
E: CROCKS: Contraindications, Repetitions (3–10), Overpressure, Communication, Knowledge, Sustain. (Mulligan lecture)

What is a "comparable sign"?
* A reproducible movement, position or activity that brings on the symptoms and can be retested after treatment
- A sign on the opposite limb
- The patient's pain score at rest only
- An X-ray finding
E: Also called asterisk sign(*). (Mulligan lecture)

What does SNAG stand for?
* Sustained Natural Apophyseal Glide
- Simple Natural Accessory Glide
- Spinal Neural Arm Glide
- Static Neutral Assisted Glide
E: NAGs = natural apophyseal glides; SNAGs = sustained; MWMs = mobilisation with movement; SMWAMs/SMWLMs = spinal with arm/leg movement. (Mulligan lecture)

If an MWM is ineffective after a few repetitions (3–6), you should:
* Stop, re-examine or abandon the technique
- Increase force until it works
- Add 20 more repetitions
- Switch to manipulation immediately
E: Practical safety tips. (Mulligan lecture)

For lateral epicondylalgia, the Mulligan lateral glide is applied while the patient:
* Grips a dynamometer or rolled towel
- Extends the shoulder
- Lies prone with the elbow straight and relaxed
- Performs wrist circles
E: Lateral glide of the proximal ulna while gripping; pain must reduce by ≥50%. (Mulligan lecture)

For the elbow lateral glide to be used as treatment, pain must reduce by at least:
* 50%
- 10%
- 25%
- 100%
E: Pain must reduce by at least 50%; it is both test and treatment. (Mulligan lecture)

Mulligan's general rule for knee MWM glide direction is:
* Lateral glide for lateral knee pain, medial glide for medial knee pain
- Always lateral glide
- Always medial glide
- Opposite glide to the painful side
E: Knee flexion with lateral/medial glide. (Mulligan lecture)

Bisset et al. (2006) found that MWM plus exercise for tennis elbow was superior to:
* Wait-and-see and corticosteroid injection
- Surgery
- Dry needling
- Ultrasound therapy
E: Bisset 2006 BMJ RCT. (Mulligan lecture)

The Mulligan talocrural MWM for restricted dorsiflexion is performed:
* In weight bearing (lunge) with a glide through the tibia/fibula
- Prone with the knee flexed and no movement
- Supine with the ankle in full plantarflexion
- Sitting with the foot off the floor
E: Patient in a weight-bearing lunge; glide applied as patient dorsiflexes. (Mulligan lecture)

Mulligan contraindications are essentially the same as those for:
* Maitland peripheral mobilisations
- Cupping
- Scar massage
- MLD
E: Fracture, malignancy, open wounds, acute injury/post-op; precautions: hypermobility, osteoporosis, irritable pain, poor comprehension. (Mulligan lecture)

Evidence for MWMs is strongest for which conditions?
* Lateral epicondylalgia, post-sprain dorsiflexion restriction, knee OA and shoulder pain
- Low back pain and fibromyalgia
- Lymphoedema and scars
- Stroke and spinal cord injury
E: Mostly short- to medium-term benefits. Pourahmadi 2018 found insufficient evidence for LBP. (Mulligan lecture)

@ Muscle Energy Techniques
Who developed Muscle Energy Technique, and when?
* Fred Mitchell Sr, DO, in 1948
- Brian Mulligan in 1972
- Emil Vodder in 1936
- Janet Travell in 1950
E: MET is an osteopathic technique developed by Fred Mitchell Sr in 1948. (MET lecture)

MET is defined as a direct manipulative procedure using:
* A voluntary contraction of the patient's muscles against a distinctly controlled counterforce
- A high-velocity thrust by the therapist
- Passive stretching only
- Suction cups on the skin
E: Voluntary contraction against controlled counterforce from a precise position and in a specific direction. (MET lecture)

Post-isometric relaxation (PIR) is based on:
* Autogenic inhibition – contracting then stretching the same muscle
- Reciprocal inhibition of the antagonist
- Lymphatic pumping
- Fascial unwinding
E: PIR = contract-relax of the same muscle (autogenic inhibition). (MET lecture)

In reciprocal inhibition MET, the patient contracts:
* The opposing muscle, which relaxes the target muscle that is then stretched
- The target muscle maximally
- No muscle at all
- Both muscles simultaneously at 100%
E: Contracting a muscle forces its reciprocal (opposing) muscle to relax. (MET lecture)

Which receptors are activated during an isometric contraction and produce autogenic inhibition?
* Golgi tendon organs
- Muscle spindles only
- Pacinian corpuscles
- Nociceptors
E: GTOs respond to tension; 1b fibres stimulate inhibitory neurons. (MET lecture)

In the course PIR protocol, how long does the therapist resist the isometric contraction?
* 7–10 seconds
- 1 second
- 60 seconds
- 3 minutes
E: Counterforce 7–10 s, relax 3–5 s, take up slack to new barrier, repeat 2–4 times. (MET lecture)

Which is a COMMON MET error highlighted in the slides?
* The patient pushes too hard
- The patient relaxes after contraction
- The therapist re-engages a new barrier
- The contraction is isometric
E: Errors: too far into barrier, patient pushes too hard, contraction too short, too few reps, movement allowed, unstable support, poor positioning. (MET lecture)

Which is a specific contraindication to MET?
* Fracture or joint instability
- Muscle tightness
- Reduced ROM
- Neck pain
E: Fracture, dislocation/instability, patient unable to understand or uncooperative. (MET lecture)

How does MET differ from static stretching?
* MET uses an isometric contraction and is not usually performed at full end range
- MET is always passive
- MET holds the stretch at greatest length for 60 s with no contraction
- They are identical
E: Static stretching has no contraction and is held at greatest length (15–60 s). (MET lecture)

The iliopsoas MET is performed in which test position?
* Thomas test position
- Prone knee bend
- Slump test
- Ober test
E: Thomas test position; untreated leg fully flexed to stabilise lumbar spine. (MET lecture)

In the Thomas test position, adding knee flexion localises the stretch to:
* Rectus femoris
- Pectineus
- TFL
- Psoas only
E: Abduction → pectineus/short adductors; adduction → TFL; knee flexion → rectus femoris. (MET lecture)

Why is MET often preferred over trigger-point therapy for subscapularis and iliopsoas?
* They are difficult muscles to trigger-point
- They have no trigger points
- MET is painful
- They cannot be stretched
E: Both described as difficult muscles to apply TrP therapy to. (MET lecture)

Research patterns for MET show that it:
* Works better as an adjunct, mostly with short-term effects
- Has strong long-term evidence as a standalone treatment
- Is harmful in low back pain
- Has never been studied in RCTs
E: Adjunct use better; immediate/short-term benefits; heterogeneous, small studies. (MET lecture)

Kang/Yang et al. (2023) meta-analysis concluded MET for hamstring flexibility is:
* More effective than no treatment and somewhat better than simple stretching
- Worse than no treatment
- Identical to placebo
- Only useful for the neck
E: 19 studies; risk of bias noted. (MET lecture)

@ Myofascial release
Myofascial release involves:
* Low-load, long-duration mechanical forces to the myofascial complex
- High-velocity, low-amplitude thrusts
- Very light skin stretching towards lymph nodes
- Suction to the skin
E: Barnes 1990: aims to restore optimum length, decrease pain and improve function. (MFR lecture)

What is the primary cell type of fascia?
* Fibroblasts
- Osteoblasts
- Neutrophils
- Adipocytes
E: Fibroblasts adapt to mechanical loading; MFR directs force to them. (MFR lecture)

Superficial fascia (hypodermis) is found:
* Between skin and muscles and contains adipose tissue
- Between adjacent muscles
- Inside bone
- Only around organs
E: Deep fascia is between adjacent muscles. (MFR lecture)

"Anatomy trains" (Myers) propose that:
* Tension in one part of a fascial line may decrease flexibility globally
- Muscles work completely independently
- Lymph flows along trains
- Bones are connected by trains of ligaments only
E: A schematic map of fascial connections. (MFR lecture)

The SIN factor stands for:
* Severity, Irritability, Nature
- Strength, Integrity, Neural
- Swelling, Inflammation, Necrosis
- Sensation, Instability, Numbness
E: Gained from the subjective assessment and used to guide treatment. (MFR lecture)

To differentiate articular from myofascial neck limitation, the therapist lifts the patient's arms; if rotation increases, the source is:
* Neuromyofascial
- Articular
- Visceral
- Vascular
E: Increase = neuromyofascial; no change = articular. (MFR lecture)

How should myofascial release techniques generally be applied?
* Very slowly, with broad contact, aiming to disrupt the fascial layers
- Fast with oil and fingertips
- Using suction cups
- With high-velocity thrusts
E: Don't always use oil; transverse or longitudinal; flat hands/knuckles; very slow. (MFR lecture)

The "pin and stretch" technique means:
* Fixing a specific point in a muscle while the patient moves that muscle
- Pinning a joint while thrusting
- Stretching without touching the muscle
- Using needles in the muscle
E: Tissues distal to the pin are affected (e.g. subscapularis with lateral rotation). (MFR lecture)

Wiewelhove et al. (2019) meta-analysis suggested foam rolling is best justified as:
* A warm-up activity rather than a recovery tool
- A recovery tool rather than warm-up
- A treatment for fractures
- Harmful to performance
E: Healey 2014: no effect on performance; Junker & Stoggl 2015: lengthens hamstrings. (MFR lecture)

Kasahara et al. (2023) found that 30 s static stretching, unlike foam rolling:
* Decreased isometric strength
- Decreased ROM
- Had no effect on ROM
- Increased tissue hardness only
E: FR recommended to increase ROM without decreasing MVC-ISO strength. (MFR lecture)

Kidd (2009) argued MFR can never be evidence-based because:
* It relies on the clinician–patient interaction and cannot be neutral
- It is too new
- It is illegal
- It uses drugs
E: Inter-rater reliability also low. (MFR lecture)

When performing SCM myofascial release, the key safety warning is:
* Don't crush the windpipe
- Never touch the clavicle
- Use maximal pressure
- Perform only in prone
E: Knuckles against anterior border of SCM while patient rotates away. (MFR lecture)

@ Manual lymphatic drainage
Lymph forms mainly because of:
* The pressure difference between the interstitium and lymphatic capillaries (hydrostatic and oncotic)
- Active pumping by the heart into lymph nodes
- Bone marrow production
- Kidney filtration
E: Hydrostatic and oncotic pressures. (MLD lecture)

Lymphangions pulsate at an average frequency of about:
* 10 per minute
- 60 per minute
- 1 per hour
- 100 per minute
E: Valve segments acting like "lymph hearts". (MLD lecture)

The right upper quadrant of the body drains via the:
* Right lymphatic duct into the right venous angle
- Thoracic duct into the left venous angle
- Cisterna chyli
- Inguinal nodes
E: Below diaphragm and left upper quadrant → thoracic duct → left venous angle. (MLD lecture)

Low-volume (mechanical) insufficiency of the lymphatic system results in:
* Lymphoedema, because transport capacity falls below normal load
- Oedema with normal transport capacity overloaded
- No swelling
- Immediate cell death only
E: High-volume (dynamic): normal capacity overwhelmed. Low-volume (mechanical): reduced capacity → lymphoedema. Safety valve: both. (MLD lecture)

Up to what proportion of breast cancer patients undergoing axillary node dissection and radiation develop lymphoedema?

- 5%
- 90%
- 1%
E: Shih et al. 2009 – up to 50%. (MLD lecture)

Who refined MLD in 1936?
* Vodder
- Leduc
- Földi
- Casley-Smith

Which hand movements belong to the Vodder method?

The course MLD sequence uses how many pumping manoeuvres on the ganglia?

- Atrophic
- Striae

A 2nd degree burn is characterised by:
* Blistered skin
- Reddening only
- Necrosis through the entire skin
- No visible change

A burn involving more than what percentage of body surface is considered serious (rule of nines)?

- 90%

The correct order of wound healing stages is:

How long does the maturation/remodelling phase last?
* 12–24 months
- A few hours
- 2 weeks
- 2–6 weeks

You should NEVER massage directly on:
* A keloid
- A healed hypertrophic scar
- A mature surgical scar
- Striae

Scar massage generally begins:

- Only after 2 years
- While scabs are present

If you plan friction or skin-rolling techniques on a scar, the lubricant should be applied:
* Afterwards
- Before, in large amounts
- Never at all
- Only on keloids
E: Tip from the scar lecture. (Scars lecture)

Which effect of scar/burn massage is supported by good evidence in burns patients?

- Complete scar removal
- Increased keloid formation
- Bone healing
E: Field 2000, Roh 2007; evidence for scar improvement remains inconsistent (Lin 2023). (Scars lecture)

When a scar becomes inflamed during treatment you should:
* Stop or decrease massage
- Increase pressure
- Add skin rolling
- Apply heat cupping
E: Considerations during treatment. Contractures/adhesions → more vigorous massage. (Scars lecture)

* A therapeutic method involving suction by creating a vacuum
- Light skin stretching towards nodes
- Isometric contraction against resistance
- Sustained pressure on a trigger point

In TCM, "sha" refers to:
* Reddish, elevated, millet-like skin rashes brought to the surface

- The bladder meridian
- A burn complication

Strong cupping should be left in place for a maximum of:

- 1 hour
- 10 seconds

"Empty/flash" cupping means:

- Cups moved with oil
- Cups over needles
